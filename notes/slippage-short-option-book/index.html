Slippage eats first: one point a fill and most of the edge is gone

In a backtest of my Nifty expiry-day book, half a point of slippage per fill removed about a third of the no-slippage result, and one point removed nearly three-quarters.

  • Options
  • Execution
  • Backtesting

An expiry-day option-selling book collects small premiums on many legs, so the cost of each fill is large next to what each trade earns.

The version of my Nifty expiry-day book I measured this on generates about 32 orders a day on its sold legs in the backtest. The backtest covers 266 expiries from July 2021 to July 2026, with statutory charges and no slippage. Hedge legs are not included.

Adding a fixed slippage to every fill in that backtest:

  • 0.5 index points per fill removed about 36% of the net result.
  • 1 point per fill removed about 72%.

The arithmetic is simple. In this test slippage is a fixed cost on every order, every expiry, so it grows in direct proportion to orders per day and points per fill, while the premium each order collects stays small. Doubling the slippage doubled the cost, from about a third of the result to nearly three-quarters of it.

The book’s notes single out the at-the-money strategies with tight stops: stops add fills, so those are the legs where the slippage assumption matters most. The no-slippage figure is best read as an upper bound, to be checked against the cost of real fills.
