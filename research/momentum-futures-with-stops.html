Nifty100 Momentum Futures Rotation

A monthly stock-futures trading strategy: every expiry, buy futures on the 20 strongest 9-month momentum stocks, hold one month, square off, repeat. This report explains the strategy from scratch and reports monthly returns for four risk-management versions (no stop-loss, plus three stop-loss designs), 2018 – mid-2026.

01What this strategy does — in plain English

Every month we bet on momentum: we buy the stocks that have been rising the most, hold them for one month using stock futures, then re-pick and repeat. The idea is simple — winners tend to keep winning for a while — and we act on it mechanically, with no discretion.

Concretely, on each monthly-expiry day we rank all 100 large-cap stocks by how much they rose over the previous 9 months, take the top 20, and put an equal amount of money into the futures contract of each. One month later, on the next expiry, we close all 20 positions, re-rank, and enter a fresh set of 20. Profits and losses are booked each month.

We trade futures instead of buying the shares outright because futures let us take the position with far less cash and are the natural instrument for a monthly trade that expires. The trade-off is a small holding cost each month (explained below), which we model as a flat drag.

02The monthly cycle

1 · RANK
On expiry day, rank all 100 Nifty100 stocks by their trailing 9-month return.
2 · SELECT
Take the top 20 strongest. Ignore the other 80.
3 · ENTER
Buy next-month futures of all 20 at the closing price — ₹5 lakh notional each.
4 · HOLD
Hold for one month. (Stop-loss rules — versions 2-4 — may exit a name early.)
5 · SQUARE OFF
On the next expiry, close all positions at the close. Book the profit/loss.
6 · REPEAT
Immediately re-rank and enter the new top 20. The cycle never breaks.

03Every parameter, defined

UniverseThe 100 current Nifty100 stocks (large caps). Daily prices come from 1-minute data aggregated to the Indian (IST) trading session.
Momentum signalTrailing 9-month price return of each stock, measured on the entry expiry: close(entry) / close(9 months earlier) − 1. Pure price momentum — no fundamentals.
Ranking & selectionSort all 100 by that 9-month return, highest first. Hold the top 20.
InstrumentSingle-stock futures, entered at the expiry close. (We use each stock's equity price as a proxy for its future, since futures history isn't loaded — see costs.)
Position size₹5,00,000 notional per stock × 20 = ₹1 crore deployed every month. Long-only.
Rebalance frequencyMonthly, on the monthly-expiry day.
Expiry-day ruleLast Thursday of the month up to Aug 2025; last Tuesday from Sep 2025 onward (NSE's rule change). If that day is a holiday, the trade rolls to the previous trading day.
Futures carry costA flat 0.5% of the ₹1 crore deployed (= ₹50,000) charged every month, representing the premium/carry paid to hold futures instead of shares.
Capital treatmentNon-compounding. A fresh ₹1 crore (₹5L × 20) is deployed every month regardless of past profits. Profits accumulate in a separate cash pile and are not added back to the base. So a year's return is simply the sum of its monthly returns, and ₹ P&L = ₹1 crore × return.
Return attributionEach trade's return is booked to the month it is squared off (entered on the previous expiry, closed on this month's expiry).
Benchmark contextChosen from an earlier study where a 9-month lookback sat on a robust performance plateau for this universe.

How to read a monthly return. Because ₹5L is deployed per stock and there are 20 of them, one month's percentage return equals the equal-weighted average return of the 20 stocks, minus the 0.5% carry. Example: if the 20 stocks averaged +4% over the month, the reported figure is +4% − 0.5% = +3.5%, i.e. +₹3.5 lakh on the ₹1 crore.

04The four versions — risk management

The base engine is identical in all four; they differ only in the stop-loss — the rule that can exit a losing position before the month ends. A stop-loss caps how much any single stock can lose you.

Version 0 — No stop-loss (baseline)

Every position is simply held from entry expiry to exit expiry. No early exits. This is the raw momentum edge, and the yardstick the stops are judged against.

Version 1 — Fixed 10% stop

Each position has a hard stop 10% below its entry price. If the stock trades down 10% intraday it's sold at the stop level; if it gaps below the stop overnight it's sold at the 9:16 AM open (often worse than −10%). Once stopped, that slot sits in cash for the rest of the month — no replacement.

Version 2 — Trailing 10% stop (on daily close)

The stop starts 10% below entry but ratchets upward every time the stock makes a new closing high — always 10% below the highest close since entry, never moving down. Checked on the daily close. This lets winners run and can lock in a profit (a stock that ran +30% then fell 10% off its peak exits around +17%).

Version 3 — Volatility-adjusted stop (ATR, fixed at entry)

Each position gets its own stop width based on its volatility: stop = entry − 3.05 × ATR(14), calibrated so the average width across names ≈ 10%. A calm stock might get a 3.5% stop, a jumpy one up to 49%. Fixed at entry, checked on close. The point: don't shake out a naturally volatile name on normal noise, but cut a quiet name that starts breaking down.

ATR (Average True Range) is a standard measure of a stock's typical daily price range — bigger ATR means a jumpier stock.

What the comparison tells us

  • V3 (volatility-adjusted) is the winner — the only stop that beats the no-stop baseline on return (+₹1.64 Cr) while still roughly halving the worst month (−11.8% vs −24.1%). It also stops the fewest positions (18%), because it fires on the right names rather than on noise.
  • The crude fixed stop (V1) costs the most return — a flat 10% is too tight for high-beta momentum names, so it whipsaws: it fires often and frequently sells stocks that then rebound.
  • The trailing stop (V2) sits in between — better than the fixed stop because it can bank gains, but it triggers most often (30%).
  • All stops protect the tail equally well (worst month ≈ −11%), which was the covid crash of March 2020. The real differentiator is how much return each gives up to get that protection — and volatility-scaling gives up none.
  • No stop design fixes momentum's core weakness: sharp trend reversals (2018, 2025) hurt every version, because the book is concentrated in whatever recently led.

07Caveats — before trusting the absolute numbers

These are research backtest results, not live trading results. The relative ranking of the four versions is trustworthy; the absolute returns are optimistic for the reasons below.
  • Survivorship bias: the universe is today's Nifty100. Stocks that dropped out of the index are excluded and current winners are baked in, which inflates returns.
  • Equity used as a futures proxy: real single-stock futures have basis, roll spreads, margin and mark-to-market dynamics that the flat 0.5%/month carry only crudely approximates.
  • The 0.5% carry is the single biggest lever — it removes ~6% a year from every version. A different carry assumption would shift all four figures together.
  • Close-based fills (V0/V2/V3) and a single stop price ignore real liquidity, slippage and impact.
  • The V3 multiplier (k = 3.05) was calibrated over the whole sample; a live version would set the ATR width from a rolling window (out-of-sample).
  • One market regime — a single ~8-year, bull-biased Indian sample. Momentum reverses violently in rotation years (2018, 2025).
